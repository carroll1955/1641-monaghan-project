Edward Blayney 

by Michael Burns


`The Nine Years War, or Tyrone’s Rebellion, was the result of the Gaelic Irish opposition to the policy pursued by the English authorities of regularising land ownership through a system of ‘surrender and regrant’, It was also a consequence of the most important political question of the age, the succession to the English and Irish crowns after the death of Elizabeth I. Add to this mixture the ambitions of Hugh O'Neill and war was inevitable. The rebellion had far-reaching consequences and lead directly to the Flight of the Earls, the Plantation of Ulster, centuries of disputes over landownership and the very definition of Irish nationality.`


`O'Neill exerted great influence in Monaghan through his alliance with Brian McHugh Og McMahon, his son-in-law. After the confiscation of Church properties by Henry VIII in the early sixteenth century, most church lands in England had passed into the hands of temporal lords. The Termon of Muckno (the lands associated with the old monastic settlement on the eastern shore of Lough Muckno, near Castleblayney), were annexed unofficially by O'Neill in 1586 when \*...he developed demesne agriculture... by placing there certain of his waged followers who would yield him rent and service.!`


`In 1585, the modem county of Monaghan was created and its territory was divided into five baronies. Church lands were confiscated and granted to English settlers. An English garrison was established in Monaghan town. The most contentious grant was to Sir Henry Bagenal, marshall of the Queen’s army: ‘the whole country and territory of Mucknoe, alias Mucknie, and all lands called the termon of Mucknoe, alias Mucknie, in co. Monaghan...’2 The animosity between Bagenal and O'Neill is well documented3 Bagenal lived at Newry and installed Eoin Duffy to administer the Termon of Muckno. For O'Neill, it was a personal insult that Muckno was in Bagenal’s possession.`


`In the Spring of 1595, the McMahons of North Monaghan, in alliance with O'Neill, surrounded the garrison at Monaghan. By May, communications with Newry were cut off and supplies were running low. Bagenal led a relief column to Monaghan. Despite a minor skirmish with O'Neill's men at Crossdall (near Middleton), he reached Monaghan town. On 27 May, after providing supplies, Bagenal led his force out of Monaghan and headed southeast towards his new lands at Muckno. O'Neill was prepared and inflicted a major defeat on him at Clontibret.4 Bagenal managed to escape back to Newry but had suffered a significant loss of authority in South Ulster.`

`Three years later, O’Neill besieged the English fort on the Blackwater river. Bagenal led an army of 300 horse and 4,000 foot north towards Armagh but O’ Neill achieved a crushing victory over him at the Battle of the Yellow Ford and Bagenal was killed. This battle caused the English authorities to panic and troops were levied on an unprecedented scale all over England to strengthen the military presence in Ireland. Among the soldiers who arrived at Newry in 1598 was a junior officer named Edward Blayney.`


`Edward Blayney was a native of Wales, born at Gregynog Hall, Tregynon, Montgomeryshire. His father, David Lloyd Blayney, had been appointed sheriff of the shire in 1577, about the time of Edward’s birth Edward’s older brother, Lewis, inherited the family estates in Wales while Edward joined the army of Elizabeth I. In the 1590s, he served with the English forces in the Low Countries. In 1598, after the defeat of Bagenal’s forces at the Battle of the Yellow Ford, he came to Ireland as an officer in the army of the Earl of Essex. Blayney was stationed at Newry.`


`Edward Blayney was a soldier of considerable merit, ‘endued with great virtue and valour’ 5 He took part in many of the important military actions of that period. In May 1600, Blayney played a leading role in the first skirmish of the Battle of Moyry Pass. Newry was the most northerly port held by the English. The main route from Dundalk to Newry by land was through the Gap of the North, also known as the Moyry Pass. George Blount, Lord Mountjoy, the Lord Deputy, arrived at Newry in the Spring of 1600 to commanded a force of over 2,000 men.`


`In May, the Earl of Southampton arrived at Faughart, near Dundalk.`

`Edward Blayney was dispatched south to escort the Earl to Newry. At the Moyry Pass on the return journey, the English Forces approached a stream known as the Four Mile Water. Blayney was in command of the vanguard of the army and, as he came near to the stream, he saw 200 of O’Neill’s men on the opposite bank. Blayney divided his force into three sections, commanding the centre section himself. He led his men across the stream under fire from the Irish and established a foothold on the northern bank. At this point the main body of the army arrived and O’Neill’s men were put to flight. Casualties were two dead and four wounded.6 By September, O'Neill had built impressive fortifications in the Moyry Pass. However, it was still possible to reach Newry via the Carlingford peninsula, which convinced O'Neill to retreat beyond the more easily defended Blackwater river.`

Mountjoy established a garrison at Monaghan under the command of two captains, Christopher St Laurence (25 horse and 150 foot), and Laurence Esmonde (150 foot).!3 These officers were soon removed from their commands. A dispute had arisen between St Laurence and Esmonde and Mountjoy recalled them to Dublin ‘that he might heal and compose the differences between them.’!4 But the real reasons for their removal were much different. Esmonde was a spy, and possibly an assassin, in the employ of the Lord Deputy. Earlier that year he had written to the Earl of Shrewsbury that “Some factions which I discovered... to be discontented with Tyrone. His later promotion to Baron Esmonde suggests his service to the Crown had been more than satisfactory.


St Laurence was a different case entirely. He was accused of stealing supplies from the English army at Kinsale. `After 1604, Edward Blayney ceased to be simply a regular soldier in an occupying army. Over the next two decades, County Monaghan would change fundamentally from a pastoral, clan-based society into a moder market economy. Because of the circumstances in which he found himself, Blayney became the major overseer of this transformation. In 1604 the conditions needed to organise a market economy did not exist in Monaghan.`

`‘The county lacked permanent towns or villages to host fairs and markets, a good road system and an effective communications network. Law and order must be administered fairly and efficiently to ensure the protection of people's lives and property. Rents and taxes must be collected and participation in the political system established. Blayney was faced with the extra complication of competing religious affiliations.`


`By 1605, the effects of the war were tackled by the English administration, Sir Arthur Chichester was appointed Lord Deputy and his approach to the new situation was that of politician rather than soldier. He took a strong line on all the issues facing his administration. The Irish currency was improved when ‘the late disorders in the Exchequer is Ireland has been altered...'27 He attempted to regularise land ownership and adopted an aggressive policy towards failure to attend the state church.28`


`Chichester began the process of improving the situation in Monaghan. On 23 July 1605, he wrote to King James seeking \*...a letter from His Majesty to convert the country of Monaghan to His Majesty's benefit, being desolate since the rebellion.’29 He intended that the work on the Monaghan fort would be resumed early in 1606, stating that he had ‘..finished the Castle of Mountjoy...and has given order to have timber and all things else in readiness for Monaghan against the spring.’2° But shortage of money delayed progress and, by March 1606, Chichester reported that ‘If I can get money I will finish that at Monaghan’ 3! Fears of a Spanish invasion of Ireland dictated policy and Edward Blayney returned to the army. In December 1625, he was stationed in Derry where he collected rents in order to fortify the town. Then the Lord Deputy ordered him to Killybegs where a Spanish landing was deemed imminent. Blayney reported:`


Foul weather and unpassable rivers kept me at Londonderry, and prevented me getting two letters for your Lordship. I quickly put the companies into garrison, and hastened from Derry through an almost impassable county to Killybegs I was stopped, however, by rain and swollen rivers and had to give up the idea.57


`In the 1620s, the Catholic Church became more confident about its position in Irish society. The number of Franciscans serving in the country trebled during this period.58 Priests who had been educated on the continent returned in increased numbers and many bishops took up residence in their own dioceses. The Gaelic nobility assisted the priests and altars were erected for the celebration of Mass.59 Blayney was given the task of policing the problem in Monaghan:`


The Papists are getting very bold. In the churches of Co Monaghan they have erected altars of lime and stone, and have resumed to celebrate mass at them, Showing that the Papists of Aghnamullen and Magheracloone in the Co Monaghan, have put up altars and celebrated mass in the churches there Order of the Irish Council that Lord Blayney... shall pull down the altars and take steps to bring the offenders before the Council.60

`A serious dispute arose in 1621 between Lord Blayney and Lord James Balfour, of Lisnaskea, County Fermanagh. Balfour proposed to marry Anne, Edward Blayney’s eldest daughter. Balfour was described as ‘an auntient man of great age’ while Anne was only fifteen years old.6' Balfour had been married twice before. Blayney was informed that Balfour had a wife still living in Scotland and was concerned until he was convinced that Balfour had divorced her. A disagreement arose between Blayney and Balfour over the dowry to be paid by Blayney. Balfour demanded £2,000 but Blayney would only offer £1,200. An agreement was reached and the marriage took place in great haste. After the wedding, Balfour reneged on the agreement and demanded the full £2,000 but Blayney refused to pay.`


`Robert Blayney was the son of Lewis Blayney of Gregynog, and nephew of Edward. He came to Ireland to assist his uncle and leased 6 townland: Tydavnet. In 1617, he married Elizabeth Blount, step-daughter of Lord Blayney and by 1624, they had four children.‘`


`Balfour accused Robert Blayney of ‘abusing his wyef both before his marriage with her and after.’63 The allegation of a relationship between Anne and Robert created a major scandal and was the reason that Balfour demanded the full dowry. Anne acknowledged the relationship but friends defended her by suggesting that she was forced to do so by her husband or that she was bewitched.`


`The matter eventually came before King James who, on 7 June 1622, appointed Sir Francis Blundell and Sir Francis Annesley «...to examine Lady Balfour of the causes that induced her to accuse herself in a matter of unchastity to her own and her parent's dishonour.’65`


`In 1624, as the English army began recruiting Lord Blayney presented a good reference for Robert who was seeking an officership in the army: ‘Robert Blany, nephew to the Lord Blany, a proper and active young gent, and one of the provost marshals, and very desirous of employment.”`
